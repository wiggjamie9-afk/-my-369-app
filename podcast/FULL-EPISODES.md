## DAD'S CODE — "Present, Not Perfect" (from the memoir)

**Description:** The heart of Dad's Code. Jamie grew up with a father whose love arrived as money, not presence — and at ten years old made a silent vow to be different. This is the story of becoming the dad he never had: four kids under four, choosing to show up through the hard days, and the wordless "air cuddle" that says everything. Being present beats being perfect, every single time. (Contains brief mention of depression and family loss — support: Lifeline 13 11 14 · Beyond Blue 1300 22 4636 · MensLine 1300 78 99 78.)

**Script:**

> Welcome to Dad's Code. I'm Jamie. And before we start, let me tell you what the code actually is — because it's not what you'd think. It's not about being the perfect dad. I'm not. The code is one word: present.
>
> Let me take you back, so you understand why that word means everything to me.
>
> I grew up with a father who worked away. His love, when it came, arrived as money — not presence. I'd see him once, maybe twice a year. I remember sitting in his car as a boy, and I had nothing to say… and he had nothing to ask. Two strangers, bound by blood. I'd watch my friends with their dads — the easy way they talked, the guidance — and I'd feel this hollow space where that was supposed to be.
>
> For a long time I was angry about it. But I understand him now. My dad was shaped by his own unbearable childhood — he lost his mum when he was just four, got shipped off, grew up with absence too. He was passing down the only thing he was ever given. Hurt people hurt people. And quiet boys carry it.
>
> So at ten years old, I made a silent vow. I didn't tell anyone. I just promised myself: if I ever have children… I will not be this. I will be there.
>
> Years later, life gave me the chance to keep that promise. My daughter was born, and I named her Indyanna — after my mum. Then came the twins, Savannah and Christopher. And then Jackson. Four children under four. Let that sink in. Nappies, bottles, sleepless nights, noise in every room. And for the first time in my whole life… I wasn't the boy left out. I was the man holding the centre.
>
> Now, here's where the code gets real. Being present sounds simple. It isn't. Because I've carried depression and anxiety for most of my adult life. There were days the weight was crushing. Days I had to drag myself up off the floor of my own mind. And on those days, I made a choice. I'd put on a smile for my kids. Not a fake one — a chosen one. Because they deserved my presence more than my pain deserved the spotlight. That smile wasn't a mask. It was a daily offering.
>
> And I showed up. First football kicks. First dance lessons. School assemblies. Every single one. My own dad likely never saw a single game I played. So I decided my kids would never wonder where I was. Work could wait. Their moments couldn't. Big smile. Biggest supporter. Win or lose, large or small — I was there.
>
> There's this thing my son Jackson and I do. At night, he whispers, "I love you, Dad." And then we press our ears together — we call it the air cuddle. No words. Just warmth, ear to ear. And in that little moment, every hard day I've had just… dissolves. That feeling — that exact feeling — is the one I never had as a child. And now it's alive between us, every night. That's the whole point. That's the code working.
>
> And here's the part that still gets me. Late in his life, my dad and I finally found each other. After twenty-five years of silence, I moved in with him. We cut wood together. Built furniture. Sat in comfortable quiet. We became, somehow, father and son again. And one ordinary Friday, something moved through me, and I said it: "I love you, Dad." He said, "I'll chat to you on Tuesday."
>
> Tuesday never came. He passed before it did. But those words did come. They live in me now — a final gift I didn't know I needed. And they taught me the most important thing I know about being a dad: say it. Show up. Don't wait for Tuesday.
>
> So that's the code. It's not perfection. It's presence. It's honesty. It's reliability. It's showing up, again and again, even on the days you can barely stand — until love becomes the very air your kids breathe.
>
> If you grew up with a hollow space where a dad should've been… you can be the one who fills it for someone else. The cycle stops with you. It stopped with me.
>
> This has been Dad's Code. Go and be present today — not perfect. Just there. I'll see you next time.

## FREQUENCY BOUND — "Why Everything Is Vibration"

**Description:** Nothing is truly still — everything, including you, is vibration. In this opening episode we explore energy, frequency, and resonance: why sound moves us so deeply, what the Solfeggio tones are, and a simple practice to tune yourself back to calm. An honest, grounded look at frequency for wellbeing (not medical advice).

**Script:**

> Welcome to Frequency Bound. I'm Jamie — and this is the show where we slow down and listen… to the thing underneath everything.
>
> Let me start with an idea that genuinely changed how I see the world. Nothing is truly still. Not this table. Not your coffee cup. Not you. Right down at the smallest level, everything is moving — humming — vibrating. Every atom, every cell, every sound, even every thought has a kind of rhythm to it. A frequency.
>
> Nikola Tesla put it like this: if you want to understand the universe, think in terms of energy, frequency, and vibration. I read that years ago, and it stuck in me like a splinter I couldn't shake. Because if everything is vibration… then maybe the way we feel — calm or anxious, heavy or light, stuck or free — isn't only about our thoughts. Maybe it's also about what we're tuned to.
>
> Now, I want to be honest with you up front. This show is an exploration, not a doctor's office. I'm not here to sell you miracle cures. Nothing I say is medical advice. If you're struggling, please see a professional — I have, and it helped. What I'm offering is something quieter: a way of paying attention.
>
> So let's talk about sound, because that's where this gets real for everyone — even the sceptics.
>
> Think about the last time a song gave you chills. Or a voice — maybe a singer, maybe someone you love — settled your whole chest, like your body exhaled before your mind caught up. That's not magic. That's resonance. Sound is a physical wave. It moves through you. And your body responds — your heart rate, your breath, your nervous system. Slow, low, steady sound tends to calm us. Sharp, fast, chaotic sound tends to wind us up. You already know this. You've felt it your whole life.
>
> There's even a word for part of it — entrainment. It's the way rhythms start to sync up. Drummers lock in together. A baby calms to a heartbeat. Your breathing slows to a gentle song. We are, whether we like it or not, tuning forks for the world around us.
>
> Now, you might have heard of the Solfeggio frequencies — a set of tones people have used for centuries, supposedly tied to different states. 396. 417. 528 — the one many call the frequency of love, or repair. 639. 741. 852. And 963, often linked to clarity and connection. And here's where I'll be straight with you again: the science on specific magic numbers is thin. I'm not going to tell you a number will heal your body. What I will tell you is what's actually true — that slowing down, breathing, and letting calm sound wash over you genuinely changes how you feel. Whether the doorway is 528 or just silence… the calm on the other side is real.
>
> For me, this isn't theory. I've lived with anxiety, depression, ADHD — a mind that ran at a thousand miles an hour my whole life. Medication helped. Understanding myself helped. But so did this. Sitting still. Letting sound carry me somewhere quieter. I couldn't play an instrument, so I started making tones and soundscapes another way, and it became a kind of prayer for me. Wordless. Just… tuning back to myself.
>
> So let's not just talk about it. Let me give you something to actually do — right now, or later tonight.
>
> Get comfortable. You don't need anything special. Let your shoulders drop. And take one slow breath in through your nose… and a longer breath out. Again. In… and slowly out. Notice you don't have to make anything happen. You're just letting yourself settle. If you've got a calming tone or a piece of soft music, play it low in the background and let your breath ride on top of it. No forcing. No fixing. Just tuning.
>
> Because here's the thing I keep coming back to. So much of our struggle is fighting ourselves — clenching, pushing, bracing. And healing, real healing, often begins the exact moment we stop. When we soften. When we let ourselves be exactly as we are, and breathe.
>
> That's the whole heart of this show. Tuning back to what's real underneath the noise. I'm so glad you're here for it.
>
> This has been Frequency Bound. Take a breath. Be gentle with yourself today. And I'll see you next time.

## THE OLD WAYS — "What the Ancients Knew"

**Description:** We're obsessed with new — new tech, new hacks, new everything. But some of the most powerful tools for a good life are thousands of years old. This opening episode rediscovers the wisdom our ancestors lived by — breath, sun, movement, stillness, and community — and why it still works today.

**Script:**

> Welcome to The Old Ways. I'm Jamie, and this is the show where we go looking — not forward, but back. Because I think we've forgotten something important.
>
> We live in a world absolutely addicted to new. New phone. New app. New hack. New supplement that's going to fix everything by Friday. And don't get me wrong — I love what technology can do. It literally helped me write my book. But somewhere along the way, in all the rushing toward the next thing, we've walked straight past tools that have been quietly working for thousands of years.
>
> Here's what fascinates me. Our ancestors didn't have science the way we do. No labs, no studies, no wearables tracking their sleep. But they had something we're losing fast: attention. They watched. They watched the sun rise and set, and built their days around it. They watched the seasons turn. They watched the body — what made it strong, what made it sick, what brought it peace. And over generations, they built practices. Not from theory… from living. From paying attention so closely that the wisdom got passed down like an heirloom.
>
> And the wild part? Modern science is now catching up — and again and again, it's confirming what the old ones already knew.
>
> Let me give you a few, just to show you what I mean.
>
> Breath. Every ancient culture knew the breath was a doorway. The yogis called it prana. The Chinese called it qi. They built entire practices around it, because they understood that how you breathe changes how you feel. And now? Science agrees completely. A slow breath out calms your nervous system. They knew that thousands of years ago, without a single study.
>
> The sun. The ancients lived by it. Up with the light, down with the dark. We call it circadian rhythm now and act like it's a discovery — but they were living it before we had a word for it. Morning light, movement through the day, rest when it's dark. Simple. Ancient. And exactly what your body still wants.
>
> Movement. They didn't go to a gym. Life was movement — walking, lifting, carrying, working the land. The body was made to move, often and naturally, and they did, because they had to. We've engineered movement out of our lives, and our bodies are quietly asking for it back.
>
> Stillness and ritual. Almost every old culture had it — a pause, a ceremony, a moment set apart. Prayer. Meditation. Sitting by a fire in silence. They knew the soul needed quiet the way the body needs food. We scroll instead. And we wonder why we feel so frayed.
>
> And community. Maybe the biggest one. The ancients didn't do life alone. They lived shoulder to shoulder — eating together, grieving together, raising children together. We've built a world where you can go a whole day without a real conversation, and it's making us unwell. The old ways knew: we heal in company, not in isolation.
>
> Now I'm not romanticising the past. Life was hard back then — brutally hard, often short. I'm not telling you to throw away your phone and live in a cave. What I'm saying is this: in our rush forward, let's not leave the good things behind. The breath. The sun. The movement. The stillness. The people. None of it costs a thing. All of it still works.
>
> So here's your one takeaway for today — the simplest old way there is. Tomorrow morning, before you reach for your phone, step outside for two minutes. Let the morning light hit your face. Take a few slow breaths. That's it. It's older than every gadget you own, and your body will thank you for it.
>
> That's what this show is about. Dusting off the old wisdom, one practice at a time, and asking: what did they know… that we forgot?
>
> This has been The Old Ways. Walk gently. And I'll see you next time.
