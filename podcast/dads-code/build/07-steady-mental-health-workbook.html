Welcome

If you're running on empty, this is for you.

If you've opened this, my guess is something's been off for a while. Maybe you can't quite name it. Maybe you're just tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix.

I know that feeling because I lived in it for years. From the outside I looked fine — working, showing up, being "the strong one." On the inside I was flat, anxious, snapping at the people I loved, and quietly convinced I was failing at the one job that mattered most: being a good dad. For a long stretch, it got dark enough that I didn't think I'd come through it.

I did come through it. Not because I toughened up, but because I finally did the opposite — I let people in, I said the thing out loud, and I started doing a few small things on purpose every day. That's what this workbook is. The handful of things I wish someone had handed me years earlier.

Here's how to use it. Each section is a short read and then a bit where you pick up a pen. The writing is the point — not because your handwriting is going to fix anything, but because getting it out of your head and onto paper is where it starts to loosen. There are no right answers. No one's marking this. You can do one section a week, or sit with the whole thing on a quiet Sunday.

You don't have to have it all together to be a good dad. You just have to keep showing up — and you can't show up for them if you're running on empty.

Go at your own pace. Be honest, even if it's only honest to the page. And remember those numbers up top are there the whole way through.

Section One

Name it.

The first time someone asked me "how are you, really?" I didn't have an answer. I'd spent so long saying "yeah, good, busy" that I'd genuinely lost track of how I actually felt. I was so used to managing everyone else's weather that I'd stopped checking my own.

Naming it sounds soft, but it's the opposite. You can't deal with a thing you won't look at. When you put a word to it — flat, wired, numb, angry, scared, exhausted — it stops being this big formless fog and becomes something you can actually do something about. Naming it is the first handhold.

So let's do a real check-in. Not the autopilot "fine." The honest one — the one you'd only say to someone you trust.

Section Two

The lie of "man up."

Most of us were handed the same instruction manual: don't complain, don't cry, sort it yourself, be the rock. "Man up." And look — there's a good intention buried in there somewhere about resilience. But as actual advice for a struggling human being, it's one of the most dangerous things we've ever told each other.

Here's the truth I learned the hard way: bottling it doesn't make it disappear. It makes it ferment. The pressure doesn't vanish — it leaks out sideways. As a short fuse with the kids. As that third beer that became a nightly thing. As lying awake at 3am with your heart going. As going quiet and distant from the very people who'd help if you let them. "Manning up" didn't make me stronger. It made me more alone, and being alone with it made everything heavier.

Strength isn't carrying it all on your own until you snap. Strength is being honest about the load and asking for a hand. The strongest blokes I know are the ones who can say "I'm not doing great" without it costing them a thing. That's not weakness leaking out. That's a man taking care of his family by taking care of himself.

Bottling it up isn't strength. It's just a slower way of breaking.

Section Three

Asking for help.

Knowing you should reach out and actually doing it are two very different things. The gap between them is usually one thing: not knowing what to say. We freeze because we're scared of being a burden, of saying it wrong, of the awkward pause. So we say nothing, and the silence wins again.

So here's the cheat code: you don't have to find the perfect words. You just have to send the first clumsy ones. The person on the other end almost never cares how polished it is — they just care that you reached out. To make it easier, here are scripts you can copy more or less word-for-word. Steal them.

Text to a mate "Hey mate, bit of a random one — I've been struggling a bit lately and just need to talk to someone who isn't going to make it weird. No advice needed, just a chat. You free for a beer or a walk this week?"
If you can't manage all that "Hey, not doing too well at the moment. Got time to talk?"
Booking the GP (phone the clinic) "Hi, I'd like to book a standard appointment with a doctor. It's about my mental health — I've been feeling really low / anxious and I want to talk through some options. Could I please have a longer appointment if that's possible?"

That last one matters. A GP can be the start of a proper plan — a Mental Health Treatment Plan in Australia can get you Medicare-subsidised sessions with a psychologist. You don't need to have it all figured out before you book. That's literally their job to help with.

Section Four

Know your warning signs.

Looking back, my low points never came out of nowhere. There were always signs — I just wasn't reading them. The sleep going first. Getting irritable over nothing. Pulling away from mates. Skipping the gym, then skipping meals, then skipping the people I loved. By the time I noticed, I was already deep in it.

The thing about your warning signs is that you can learn them. They're personal — yours might be a short fuse, mine might be going quiet. When you know your own early signals, you get to act early, while it's still a small thing. That's the whole game: catching it at a 4 instead of a 9.

Then there's a simple safety plan — a page you write now, while you're steady, for the version of you that isn't. Think of it like the emergency card on the back of a door. You hope you never need it. You're very glad it's there if you do. Filling this in is a calm, sensible thing to do, the same as knowing where the fire exit is.

Section Five

Small daily anchors.

When you're struggling, "fix your whole life" is useless advice. You don't have the fuel for a grand plan, and the size of it just makes you feel worse. What actually pulled me up wasn't a transformation. It was three tiny things I did most days, whether I felt like it or not.

I call them anchors. They're small on purpose. Ten minutes outside in the morning light. A glass of water and a real breakfast before coffee. A two-minute message to a mate. Making the bed. None of these are impressive. That's exactly why they work — they're small enough to do on a bad day, and doing them gives you a little handhold of "I did something." Stack a few of those and you've got a day that holds together instead of one that falls apart.

You don't climb out all at once. You climb out one small, boring, repeated thing at a time.

Pick anchors that are almost too easy. If it feels ambitious, shrink it. "Go for a run" becomes "put my shoes on and step outside." The win is in the doing, not the size.

Section Six

Your person.

If you take one thing from this whole workbook, make it this: don't do it alone. The single biggest shift for me wasn't a technique. It was one person who knew the real story and checked in on me. Just one. You don't need a crowd — you need one mate, one brother, one mentor, one good human who knows what's going on and won't let you disappear.

The trick is to set this up before you're in the hole, because that's exactly when reaching out feels impossible. Decide now who your person is. Tell them they're your person — actually say it: "Hey, I'm going through some stuff and I might lean on you a bit. That okay?" Almost everyone says yes, and most feel honoured you asked. Then agree on a way they can check in on you, so it's not all on your shoulders to reach out.

And here's the quiet bonus: when you let one person in, you give them permission to let you in too. That's how the whole "strong silent dad" thing finally breaks — one honest conversation at a time.

Where to from here

You're still here. That counts.

If you've made it to this page — even if half the lines are still blank — you've already done the hardest part, which is to stop pretending you're fine and start looking at it honestly. That's not a small thing. That's the turn.

I won't tell you it's all uphill from here, because some days will still be heavy. But I will tell you this, because I lived it: it does get lighter. The fog lifts. You laugh again, properly. You're present with your kids in a way you weren't when you were just surviving. The man who genuinely believed he wouldn't come through it is the one writing this to you now — steadier, lighter, and very glad he stuck around. If I can get there, so can you.

Come back to these pages whenever you need them. Redo a section. Fill in the safety plan again. And whatever you do — keep one person close, and keep showing up.

You were never meant to carry this on your own. Present, not perfect — that's the whole job.
