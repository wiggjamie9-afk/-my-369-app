## EP 02 — "The Five Minutes That Matter"

**Description:** You think being a good dad takes hours you don't have. It doesn't. It takes five real minutes — phone down, eyes up. Jamie on why small, present moments beat big, distracted ones every time, and the tiny daily habit that changes how your kids feel about you. (Wellbeing, not medical advice.)

**Tags:** fatherhood, present parenting, dad, busy dad, connection, family

**Script:**

> Welcome to Dad's Code. I'm Jamie. And today I want to take the pressure off you. Because I think a lot of us are carrying around a lie about what it takes to be a good dad.
>
> The lie is this: that it takes hours. That to really be there for your kids, you need long lazy days, big trips, hours of undivided time you simply do not have — not with work, not with the bills, not with the sheer noise of ordinary life. And because we can't give them the hours, a lot of us quietly give up trying. We tell ourselves we'll make it up to them later. When things calm down. When there's more time.
>
> But here's what I've learned, raising four kids under four, on some of the hardest days of my life. Kids don't remember the length of the time. They remember the quality of the attention. And attention doesn't take hours. It takes minutes. Five real minutes — phone down, eyes up, fully there — beats a whole day of being in the same room but a thousand miles away.
>
> Let me say that again, because it's the whole episode. Five present minutes beats five distracted hours. Every single time.
>
> Think about it from their side. A little kid doesn't experience your love as a total number of hours logged. They feel it in moments. The moment you crouch down to their level and actually listen to the very long, very boring story about the thing that happened at school. The moment you stop, and look, and say "show me." That moment lands in them like a stone in a pond — and the ripples go on for years.
>
> And here's the trap of the phone, because I have to be honest about it. Being physically present isn't the same as being present. I've done it — sat on the floor with my kids, body there, mind scrolling, saying "mmhmm" to a face I wasn't really looking at. Kids know. They always know. There's nothing that says "you don't matter right now" quite like a parent whose eyes keep sliding back to a screen. That's not connection. That's just proximity.
>
> So here's the practice. One a day. Just one. Pick five minutes — bath time, the drive to school, the last few minutes before lights out — and make it sacred. Phone in the other room. Not in your pocket. The other room. And just… be with them. Follow whatever they want to talk about. Ask one real question and actually wait for the answer. That's it. Five minutes of the realest you you've got.
>
> Do that, and something shifts. Not overnight. But over weeks. Your kid starts to feel like the most interesting person in the room when they're with you — because for five minutes, they are. That feeling becomes the ground they stand on.
>
> My own dad never gave me those five minutes. I sat in his car as a boy with nothing to say and a father with nothing to ask. I felt that hollow space for years. So I know, from the inside, what its absence does. And I know what its presence can do — because I've watched it grow between me and my own kids, five minutes at a time.
>
> You don't need more hours. You've already got five minutes in there somewhere. You just have to give them all of you.
>
> That's the code today. Not more time. Better minutes. Go and find your five today — phone down, eyes up. I'll see you next time.

## EP 03 — "Don't Wait for Tuesday"

**Description:** The hardest, most important lesson Jamie ever learned about being a dad — and it came from his own father, too late. Why you should say the thing, show up, and love out loud today, because "later" is never promised. (Contains reference to family loss and grief — support: Lifeline 13 11 14 · Beyond Blue 1300 22 4636 · MensLine 1300 78 99 78.)

**Tags:** fatherhood, grief, legacy, love, dad, saying I love you

**Script:**

> Welcome to Dad's Code. I'm Jamie. This one's close to the bone, so I'll just tell it to you straight.
>
> For twenty-five years, my father and I didn't really speak. There was too much between us — too much absence, too much hurt. He'd been a dad who worked away, whose love arrived as money instead of presence, and I'd grown up with a hollow space where he should have been. And for a long time, I was angry about it.
>
> But late in his life, we found our way back. I moved in with him. And it wasn't dramatic — it was quiet. We cut wood together. We built furniture. We sat in comfortable silence, two men who'd finally stopped fighting the past. Somehow, against all odds, we became father and son again.
>
> And one ordinary Friday, something rose up in me, and I said the thing I'd never said. I said, "I love you, Dad." And he looked at me, and he said, "I'll chat to you on Tuesday."
>
> Tuesday never came. He passed before it did.
>
> Now I could have carried that as a wound. The words he didn't quite say back. But I don't. Because I said mine. On the Friday. Not the Tuesday. And those words — "I love you, Dad" — they live in me now as the thing I got right. Not the thing I missed. Imagine if I'd waited. Imagine if I'd told myself, "next time, when it feels less awkward, when the moment's better." I'd have carried that silence for the rest of my life.
>
> And that taught me the most important thing I know about being a dad, and honestly about being a human being. Say it now. Don't wait for Tuesday.
>
> Because here's what we do, isn't it. We assume there's time. We assume the people we love will always be there to receive the thing we keep meaning to say. We put off the real conversation, the apology, the "I'm proud of you," the "I love you" — because it feels easier tomorrow. And we build whole lives out of things we meant to say and didn't.
>
> With your kids, this matters more than anything. Tell them you love them, out loud, today. Tell them you're proud of them — not for what they did, for who they are. Say the soft thing. Say it even when it feels awkward, even when they roll their eyes, even when you didn't grow up with it said to you and the words feel strange in your mouth. Say it anyway. You are teaching them that love is something you speak, not just something you assume.
>
> My son Jackson and I, we have this thing at night. He whispers, "I love you, Dad," and we press our ears together — we call it the air cuddle. No words after that. Just warmth. And every time, I think: this is the thing I never had, and here it is, alive, because someone decided to say it out loud.
>
> So here's your challenge, and it's a simple one, but do it today — not Tuesday. Tell someone you love them, plainly, with nothing attached. Your kid. Your partner. Your own dad, if you still can. Pick up the phone. Say the words you've been saving for a better moment. This is the better moment. It's the only one you're guaranteed.
>
> Don't wait for Tuesday. That's the code. I'll see you next time — and I mean that.

## EP 04 — "Running on Empty"

**Description:** For the dad who's carrying more than he lets on. Jamie talks honestly about depression, anxiety, and the "chosen smile" — how he learned to show up for his kids on the days he could barely stand, and why asking for help is the strongest thing a father can do. (Mental health content — not a substitute for professional care. Support: Lifeline 13 11 14 · Beyond Blue 1300 22 4636 · MensLine 1300 78 99 78.)

**Tags:** mens mental health, dad mental health, depression, anxiety, burnout, fatherhood

**Script:**

> Welcome to Dad's Code. I'm Jamie. And before anything else, if today is one of the heavy ones for you — I'm glad you're here. Stay with me.
>
> I've carried depression and anxiety for most of my adult life. I want to say that plainly, because too many of us dads never do. There were days the weight was genuinely crushing. Days I had to drag myself up off the floor of my own mind just to function. From the outside, you'd never have known. And that's exactly the problem I want to talk about.
>
> Because there's this idea we hand to men, and especially to dads: that we're the strong ones. The providers. The steady rock everyone else leans on. And so we learn to hide the cracks. We say "I'm fine" on a loop. We carry it silently, and we call that strength. But it isn't strength. It's just silence. And silence is heavy, and it's dangerous, and it's lonely.
>
> Now here's the part people find contradictory, and I need to explain it carefully. On my hardest days, I still put on a smile for my kids. And someone might hear that and think — isn't that faking it? Isn't that hiding?
>
> No. And here's the difference. That smile was never a mask to fool anyone. It was a choice — a chosen smile. On the days the black dog had me, I made a decision that my kids deserved my presence more than my pain deserved the spotlight. That smile was a daily offering. It said: I'm still here, I've still got you, even today. That's not denial. That's a father deciding, over and over, to show up.
>
> But — and this is the crucial but — the chosen smile is what you give your kids. It is not the whole story you tell yourself. Because a smile you put on for them cannot be the only thing holding you up. If it is, you will break. I know, because I nearly did.
>
> What actually held me up was the stuff I did in the quiet. I got help. I saw professionals — and it helped. I came to understand my own mind, the anxiety, the ADHD, the way I was wired. I found things that steadied me: movement, sleep, honest conversation, and later, sitting still and letting calm wash over me. None of it was a magic cure. All of it, together, kept me standing.
>
> So this is what I want to say to you, dad to dad. You can be the rock for your kids and still need someone to be a rock for you. Those two things are not in conflict. The strongest thing I have ever done as a father was not gritting my teeth and carrying it alone. It was admitting I couldn't, and asking for help.
>
> You cannot pour from an empty cup. You know that. You say it about other people. Now say it about yourself. Check your own levels. Tell one person the truth this week — not the "I'm fine," the real thing. Book the appointment you keep putting off. Move your body, even just a walk. Protect your sleep like it matters, because you matter.
>
> And if you're right down at the bottom today — please reach out. Lifeline, thirteen eleven fourteen. Beyond Blue. MensLine. There are people who will pick up. Asking is not weakness. It's the bravest move on the board.
>
> Your kids don't need a perfect dad who never struggles. They need a present one who's still here. Look after the man who's holding them up. That's the code today. Be gentle with yourself. I'll see you next time.

## EP 05 — "Diagnosed Late"

**Description:** Jamie was diagnosed with ADHD and dyslexia in his forties — and everything suddenly made sense. For every dad who's felt "lazy," "scattered," or "not enough" their whole life: this is the relief of finally understanding your own brain, and why systems beat willpower every time. (Wellbeing, not medical advice.)

**Tags:** ADHD, adult ADHD, late diagnosis, dyslexia, neurodivergent dad, fatherhood

**Script:**

> Welcome to Dad's Code. I'm Jamie. Today's for anyone who's spent their whole life feeling like they were somehow failing at being a person — and never quite knew why.
>
> I was diagnosed with ADHD and dyslexia in my forties. Late. And I want to tell you what that moment was actually like, because it wasn't what I expected. It wasn't bad news. It was the opposite. It was like someone finally handed me the instruction manual to a machine I'd been operating blind my entire life.
>
> Because before that, here's the story I'd been told about myself — the story I'd come to believe. That I was lazy. Scattered. That I started things and didn't finish them. That I could focus like a laser on the thing I loved and completely fall apart on the thing I was supposed to do. That I was too much and not enough, both at once. I thought that was a character flaw. I thought it was me failing.
>
> And then I learned: no. That's a brain. A specific kind of brain, that works differently — not worse, differently. And suddenly all these things I'd beaten myself up about for forty years had a name and a reason. The shame started to lift. Not all at once. But it lifted.
>
> If that's you — if you've carried that low hum of "what's wrong with me" your whole life — I want you to hear this: maybe nothing is wrong with you. Maybe you've just been running the wrong software for your hardware, and blaming yourself for the crashes.
>
> Now here's the most practical thing I learned, and it changed everything. For a brain like mine, willpower is not the answer. I spent years trying to white-knuckle my way to being organised, being consistent, being "normal." And I failed every time, because willpower runs out. It's a candle, not the sun.
>
> What actually works is systems. Systems over willpower. Instead of trying to remember, I build something that remembers for me. Instead of relying on motivation to show up, I make the thing automatic, or so easy I can't skip it. I put the reminder where I'll trip over it. I make the routine so simple it survives a bad day. I stopped trying to change my brain, and started building a life that works with it.
>
> And this matters as a dad in two directions. One — when you stop fighting your own wiring, you've got so much more calm and energy left for your kids. And two — if one of your kids turns out to be wired like you, you will see it early. You'll recognise it. And instead of passing down the shame you were handed, you'll be able to say the words you needed to hear: there's nothing wrong with you. Your brain just works differently. Let's find the way that works for you.
>
> That right there — that's the cycle breaking.
>
> So here's your takeaway. If you've suspected for years that your brain works differently, look into it. Talk to a professional. Understanding yourself is not a label to be scared of — it's a key. And whatever your wiring, stop relying on willpower to fix things. Build one small system this week instead. One reminder, one routine, one thing made automatic. Let the system carry what your memory keeps dropping.
>
> You're not lazy. You're not broken. You've just been playing a game without knowing the rules. Now you know. That's the code today. I'll see you next time.

## EP 06 — "How Many Bedtimes Left?"

**Description:** A question that will change how you spend today: how many bedtimes do you actually have left with your kids before they're grown? Jamie on the maths of childhood, the "936 weeks," and why seeing the number is the gut-punch that turns an ordinary night into a gift. (Wellbeing, not medical advice.)

**Tags:** fatherhood, mindful parenting, time with kids, present dad, family, dad

**Script:**

> Welcome to Dad's Code. I'm Jamie. I want to ask you a question today that might sting a little. But stay with me, because on the other side of the sting is something beautiful.
>
> Here's the question. How many bedtimes do you have left with your kids? Not in total, over their whole lives — I mean the real ones. The ones where they're little, and they want you to tuck them in, and read the story, and stay a minute longer. How many of those have you actually got left before they're too big, too cool, too grown for it?
>
> Most of us never do that maths. And there's a reason we don't. It's because the days feel endless. Especially the hard ones — the sleepless nights, the tantrums, the same bedtime battle for the hundredth time. In the thick of it, it feels like it will last forever. So we rush it. We hurry them through the bath, half-read the story, one eye on the door, thinking about everything else we've got to do once they're finally down.
>
> But here's the truth that maths reveals. Childhood is short. Brutally short. There's this idea that a whole childhood — birth to eighteen — is about 936 weeks. That's it. Nine hundred and thirty-six. And if your kid is, say, six years old, you've already spent more than 300 of them. They're not coming back. The weeks don't refill.
>
> Now I'll be honest — when I first sat with that number, it hit me hard. It's confronting. But it didn't leave me sad. It left me awake. Because a thing you have a limited number of is a thing you stop wasting. Nobody rushes the last few bites of a meal they love. Nobody hurries through the final page of a book they don't want to end.
>
> That's what the number does. It takes an ordinary Tuesday-night bedtime — the one you were about to rush — and it shows you what it actually is. It's not a chore. It's one of a countable, running-out number of nights where a small person who adores you wants nothing in the world except for you to stay a little longer. Seen like that, how do you rush it?
>
> My own dad barely saw my childhood at all. He worked away; I saw him once or twice a year. He didn't count the bedtimes, because he wasn't there for them. And I promised myself, at ten years old, that I would be different. That my kids would never wonder where I was. So I show up for the bedtimes. Even on the empty days. Especially on the empty days. Because I've done the maths, and I know what they're worth.
>
> So here's what I want you to do tonight. Just tonight. When it's bedtime, don't rush it. Read the story like it's the last one. Stay the extra minute. Press your ear to theirs if that's your thing. Look at their face and actually take the picture in your mind, the way you'll wish you had in twenty years. You will never regret the night you slowed down. You'll only ever regret the ones you hurried.
>
> The days are long, but the weeks are few. Spend tonight like it counts — because it does. That's the code. Go and have your bedtime. I'll see you next time.
